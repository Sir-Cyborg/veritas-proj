1. Introduction

ICT risk is the risk of financial loss, service disruption, regulatory breach, or
reputational damage resulting from inadequate or failed internal processes, people, systems,
or from external events affecting ICT. In a banking context, ICT risk management must
support operational resilience, information security, and sound outsourcing practices.

This policy defines the governance model, minimum processes, and reporting requirements for
identifying, assessing, treating, monitoring, and reporting ICT risk within Corporate
Banking Group (CBG).

2. Objectives

The objectives of this policy are to:

- Ensure ICT risks are identified and assessed consistently using a common taxonomy and methodology.

- Align ICT risk treatment with CBG risk appetite and operational resilience objectives.

- Support regulatory expectations for ICT and third-party risk management and audit readiness.

- Provide management with timely visibility into key ICT risk exposures, control effectiveness, and remediation status.

3. Scope

This policy applies to:

- All ICT services, applications, infrastructure components, and supporting processes used by CBG.

- All change programs and projects introducing new technology or materially changing existing services.

- All third-party ICT services and outsourcing arrangements, including cloud and managed services.

The policy covers risks related to information security, availability, continuity, change
and release, data management, technology obsolescence, and supplier dependency.

4. Risk Governance

4.1 Three Lines of Defense (3LoD)

CBG adopts a three lines of defense model for ICT risk:

- First line (ICT delivery and operations): owns risks and controls; implements remediation.

- Second line (ICT Risk Management and Information Security): defines methodology, challenges assessments, monitors compliance.

- Third line (Internal Audit): provides independent assurance on the effectiveness of controls and governance.

4.2 Committees and Reporting

ICT Risk Management reports key risk indicators (KRIs), top risks, and remediation progress
to the Operational Risk Committee and relevant technology governance forums at least
monthly. Material risks are escalated promptly.

5. Risk Management Lifecycle Requirements

5.1 Identification

Risks must be identified via multiple sources including architecture reviews, threat
modeling, incident/problem trends, vulnerability findings, audit issues, supplier
assessments, and business impact analyses.

5.2 Assessment

Risks must be assessed for inherent risk and residual risk. Assessment must consider
likelihood and impact across dimensions relevant to banking: customer harm, financial loss,
regulatory impact, data confidentiality/integrity, and service availability.

5.3 Treatment

Treatment options include mitigate, transfer, avoid, and accept. Mitigations must be
documented as control improvements or remediation actions with owners, deadlines, and
measurable outcomes.

5.4 Monitoring and Review

Risks must be reviewed at least quarterly, or more frequently for high residual risks. Risk
status must be updated based on control changes, new threats, incident trends, and supplier
performance.

5.5 Reporting

Management reporting must include: top risks by service/domain, overdue remediation actions,
risk acceptances and expiries, key KRIs (e.g., patch compliance, critical vulnerabilities,
major incidents, resilience test results), and concentration risks related to critical
suppliers.

6. Risk Appetite and Acceptance

CBG defines ICT risk appetite through quantitative and qualitative statements approved by
executive management. ICT Risk Management must translate risk appetite into operational
thresholds (e.g., maximum time-to-patch for critical systems, maximum allowed unsupported
software, acceptable downtime) and ensure those thresholds are embedded into standards and
operational dashboards.

Risk acceptance must be time-bound and supported by a clear rationale. For high residual
risks affecting critical services, acceptance requires approval at an appropriate senior
level and evidence that compensating controls have been evaluated.

7. Key ICT Risk Domains and Minimum Controls

CBG maintains a risk taxonomy that includes (at minimum) the following domains and expected
controls:

- Cybersecurity risk: identity controls, hardening, vulnerability management, monitoring, incident response.

- Resilience and continuity risk: backup/recovery, disaster recovery testing, capacity management, high availability architecture.

- Change and release risk: change governance, segregation of duties, testing, rollback plans, emergency change control.

- Data risk: classification, retention, encryption, data loss prevention, integrity controls for transactional data.

- Obsolescence risk: lifecycle management, upgrade planning, vendor supportability tracking.

- Third-party risk: due diligence, contract controls, exit planning, concentration management.

8. Documentation and Evidence

Risk assessments must be recorded in the risk register or approved tooling. Each high or
critical risk must have supporting evidence, including control descriptions, test results
where applicable, and linkage to incidents, vulnerabilities, changes, and audit findings.
Evidence must be retained according to record retention requirements and be accessible for
audits and regulatory inspections.

9. Non-Compliance

Non-compliance with this policy must be documented and remediated. Repeated non-compliance
may result in escalation to the CIO and CRO and may trigger enhanced monitoring or audit
review. Where non-compliance increases risk beyond appetite, compensating controls or
service restrictions may be required.
